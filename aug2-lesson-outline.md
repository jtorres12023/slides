# July 27-August 2 Lesson Plan

## I Am Doing a Great Work

**Scriptures:** Ezra 1; 3-7; Nehemiah 2; 4-6; 8

**Central message:** The Lord did more than bring His people back to Jerusalem. He helped them rebuild a covenant community centered on His house, protected by wise action, and guided by His word. He can likewise strengthen us to continue His work despite opposition and distraction.

**Key phrase:**

> "I am doing a great work, so that I cannot come down." - Nehemiah 6:3

## The Story at a Glance

This lesson covers events that unfolded over nearly a century, not one continuous building project.

- In 586 BC, Babylon destroyed Jerusalem and the temple and carried many of the people away.
- In 539 BC, Persia conquered Babylon.
- Around 538 BC, Cyrus allowed Jewish exiles to return and rebuild the temple.
- In 515 BC, the rebuilt temple was completed and dedicated.
- About 60 years later, Ezra came to Jerusalem to teach the law of Moses and help the people return to their covenants.
- About a decade after Ezra's arrival, Nehemiah came to rebuild Jerusalem's walls and gates.

Three leaders contributed to different parts of the restoration:

- **Zerubbabel:** helped rebuild the temple.
- **Ezra:** taught the law and called the people back to covenant faithfulness.
- **Nehemiah:** organized the rebuilding of the city walls and helped restore Jerusalem as a secure community.

The sequence matters: **the house of the Lord, the word of the Lord, and a people prepared to live in covenant with the Lord.**

## Recommended 40-45 Minute Flow

- **5 minutes:** Introduce the exile and the return to Jerusalem
- **8 minutes:** Cyrus, Zerubbabel, and the rebuilding of the temple
- **6 minutes:** Ezra prepares his heart to seek, do, and teach
- **12 minutes:** Why Jerusalem's wall mattered and how Nehemiah responded
- **7 minutes:** Opposition and "I cannot come down"
- **5 minutes:** The public reading of the law and covenant renewal
- **2 minutes:** Testimony and invitation

If time is short, focus on sections 1, 3, 4, and 5.

## Opening: Returning to a Ruined City

Invite the class to imagine returning to their homeland after their family had been gone for generations. The temple is burned, much of the city is in ruins, and the walls and gates that once protected it are broken.

Ask:

**What would Jerusalem need to become a faithful community again?**

Explain that the answer in Ezra and Nehemiah unfolds in stages. The people needed a place to worship, the word of God to guide them, physical security, trustworthy leadership, and renewed commitment to their covenants.

## 1. The Lord Makes a Return Possible

**Read:** Ezra 1:1-3, 5

**Listen for:** Who helped the work begin?

### Historical context

The Jewish people had lived under Babylonian captivity for about 70 years. When Persia conquered Babylon, Cyrus followed a policy that allowed displaced peoples to return to their homelands and restore their places of worship. Ezra teaches that the Lord worked through this Persian king, even though Cyrus was not an Israelite, to fulfill His purposes and earlier prophecy.

Returning was not easy or automatic. Many families had lived their whole lives in Babylon. Those who went to Jerusalem were choosing uncertainty and difficult work in order to restore the worship and community of God's covenant people.

### Teaching points

- God had not forgotten His people during their captivity.
- The Lord can accomplish His purposes through people we may not expect.
- The Lord stirred Cyrus to authorize the return and stirred the people to act.
- Inspiration often leads to a decision that still requires courage and work.

### Discuss

- What does it mean for the Lord to "stir" someone's heart?
- Why might some people have hesitated to return?
- When has God used an unexpected person to help His work?

**Transition:** Returning to Jerusalem was only the beginning. The first priority was restoring the temple.

## 2. Rebuilding the Temple Restored Worship

**Read:** Ezra 3:10-13; consider summarizing Ezra 6:15-22

**Listen for:** Why did some people rejoice while others wept?

### Historical context

The Babylonians had plundered and burned Solomon's temple. For the returning exiles, rebuilding it meant that worship, sacrifice, holy days, and covenant life could again be centered in the house of the Lord.

The new temple was approximately the same size as Solomon's temple but was not as grand, and the ark of the covenant was no longer in the Holy of Holies. Older people who remembered the former temple may have wept over what had been lost, even as others shouted because something sacred was being restored.

The work also took years. Opposition delayed construction, and at times the people lost momentum. Prophets such as Haggai and Zechariah encouraged them to begin again.

### Teaching points

- The temple showed that the Lord had not abandoned His covenant people.
- Joy and sorrow can be present in the same sacred experience.
- Restoration does not always mean recreating the past exactly; it means faithfully receiving what the Lord provides now.
- The house of the Lord points us to Jesus Christ, covenants, repentance, and eternal life.

### Discuss

- Why did rebuilding the temple matter?
- What might the older generation's tears teach us about loss, memory, and hope?
- Why do temples bring joy today?

**Transition:** A temple had been restored, but buildings alone could not restore a covenant people. They also needed to understand and live God's word.

## 3. Ezra Prepared His Heart

**Read:** Ezra 7:6, 9-10, 27-28

**Listen for:** What prepared Ezra to help the people?

### Historical context

Ezra arrived decades after the temple was completed. He was both a priest and a scribe. Scribes preserved, studied, and taught sacred texts; many people did not own personal copies of scripture or read them privately. Ezra therefore had an important role in helping an entire community understand God's law.

Ezra also had authorization and material support from the Persian king. The repeated phrase about "the good hand" of God upon him shows how Ezra interpreted that royal support: the Lord was opening the way for the work.

### Teaching points

- Ezra "prepared his heart" before trying to reform or teach others.
- Ezra's pattern was to seek God's law, do it, and then teach it.
- Knowledge of scripture becomes powerful when it leads to faithful living.
- The Lord's hand may be visible in preparation, relationships, permission, resources, and strength.

### Discuss

- Why does the order "seek, do, and teach" matter?
- How is knowing a commandment different from living it?
- Where have you seen "the good hand" of God in your service?

**Transition:** Ezra helped restore the people's covenant understanding. Nehemiah later faced another unfinished part of Jerusalem's restoration: its walls and gates.

## 4. Why Jerusalem's Wall Mattered

**Read:** Nehemiah 2:3-5, 11-18

**Listen for:** What did Nehemiah observe, feel, and do?

### Historical context

In the ancient world, a city without walls and working gates was exposed. Raiders or armies could enter, property and food stores were vulnerable, and residents could not defend themselves effectively. Broken walls also made it harder for Jerusalem to grow as a stable center of worship, trade, government, and community life.

The wall therefore mattered in several connected ways:

- **Physical security:** It protected families, homes, food, and the temple area from attack.
- **Community stability:** People were more likely to settle, work, and raise families in a city that could be defended.
- **Economic strength:** Controlled gates allowed trade and ordinary city life to function more securely.
- **Religious and civic identity:** Jerusalem could again function as the center of Jewish worship and community under Persian rule.
- **Freedom from disgrace:** The ruined wall was visible evidence of Jerusalem's defeat and continuing weakness. Rebuilding it showed that the community was no longer abandoned and helpless.

This also explains the opposition. Sanballat, governor of Samaria, Tobiah, an Ammonite official, and other regional leaders feared that a secure and influential Jerusalem could reduce their own power and control. Their resistance was political as well as personal.

### Teaching points

- Nehemiah was the Persian king's cupbearer, a trusted court position that gave him access to the king.
- He prayed before making his request, then asked for authorization, letters, and timber.
- After arriving, he quietly inspected the damage before announcing a plan.
- His faith included prayer, careful observation, planning, obtaining resources, and inviting others to work.
- "Let us rise up and build" turned a discouraging problem into a shared responsibility.

### Discuss

- How did Nehemiah combine faith and preparation?
- Why did the wall matter beyond military safety?
- What can Nehemiah teach us about acting on a need?

## 5. Building While Facing Opposition

**Read:** Nehemiah 4:6, 9, 14, 17-18; 6:2-3, 9, 15-16

**Listen for:** How did the people respond to ridicule, danger, fear, and distraction?

### Historical context

Nehemiah's opponents first mocked the project, then threatened violence, spread accusations, and tried to lure Nehemiah away from Jerusalem. The builders had to organize guards and remain ready to defend themselves while continuing the work. The wall was completed in only 52 days, an achievement so remarkable that even their enemies recognized that God had helped them.

The invitation to meet at the plain of Ono was not an innocent request. Nehemiah understood that leaving Jerusalem could expose him to harm and interrupt the work. His response, "I am doing a great work, so that I cannot come down," was a statement of discernment and priority.

### Teaching points

- Having "a mind to work" meant shared commitment, not the absence of fear or fatigue.
- The people prayed and set a watch. Trust in God and wise precautions worked together.
- Nehemiah reminded the people to remember the Lord when fear threatened their resolve.
- Opposition changed its form: ridicule, threats, rumors, distraction, and deception.
- Nehemiah did not give every accusation or invitation equal attention.
- His prayer, "O God, strengthen my hands," shows dependence on God without surrendering responsibility.

### Discuss

- How can we distinguish a real need from a distraction?
- What does "we prayed ... and set a watch" teach us?
- What deserves an "I cannot come down" from us?

**Transition:** Finishing the wall made the city secure, but Nehemiah 8 shows the real purpose of the restoration. The people gathered not merely inside a protected city, but around the word of God.

## 6. Understanding the Law Completed the Restoration

**Read:** Nehemiah 8:1-3, 5-12

**Listen for:** What did the people and their teachers do, and what changed when the people understood?

### Historical context

Copies of scripture were scarce, and many people could not read. Public reading was therefore essential. Ezra read from the law of Moses while Levites helped the people understand it, possibly by explaining its meaning and translating Hebrew into the Aramaic language many of them spoke.

The people wept, likely because they recognized how far they and earlier generations had fallen short. Ezra and Nehemiah did not tell them that their sorrow was meaningless. They taught that this holy day was a time to rejoice, share with those in need, and find strength in the Lord.

The scene completes the lesson's historical movement. Nearly a century after the first return from exile, the temple stood, the walls were secure, and the people were gathered to hear, understand, and respond to God's word. The restoration was ultimately about restoring a people to covenant faithfulness.

### Teaching points

- The people wanted to hear the law; Ezra did not force the gathering upon them.
- Good teaching does more than repeat words. It helps people understand their meaning.
- Conviction can lead to sorrow, but godly sorrow should move us toward repentance and joy in the Lord.
- "The joy of the Lord is your strength" connects covenant faithfulness with spiritual strength.
- The people acted on what they learned by celebrating and sharing with those who had nothing prepared.

### Discuss

- Why did understanding God's law matter as much as rebuilding Jerusalem?
- How is hearing scripture different from understanding it?
- How can repentance lead to joy?

## Closing Discussion

Return to the opening question:

**What did Jerusalem need restored to become a faithful community?**

Draw out the connected answers:

- A return to the promised land
- The temple and worship of the Lord
- God's law understood and lived
- Walls that gave the community security and stability
- People willing to work together
- Faithfulness despite opposition
- Renewed joy and covenant commitment

Then ask:

**What great work has the Lord given us? What will help us remain faithful?**

## Closing Testimony

Ezra and Nehemiah testify that the Lord does not abandon His covenant people. He opened a way for them to return, inspired leaders with different gifts, strengthened them through opposition, and brought them back to His house and His word. Jesus Christ continues His work of restoration in us. He can rebuild what has been damaged, strengthen our hands, and help us become a people who know, live, and rejoice in His word.

Invite each person to identify one important work the Lord has placed before them and complete this sentence privately:

> **Because this is a great work, I will __________ and I will not come down for __________.**
